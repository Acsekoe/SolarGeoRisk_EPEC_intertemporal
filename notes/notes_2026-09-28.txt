[Results / prices] Even the lowest observed equilibrium price in EU, US, AF and ROW lies above the planner price in every period. In 2040 the medians are 187-221 USD/kW against 99-105 USD/kW under the planner (about 2.1x), so no equilibrium comes close to the competitive outcome in these regions. The ROW median price (187.3) coincides with ROW's own manufacturing cost (187.2), i.e. in the median profile ROW's price is set by its domestic supply at cost.

[Results / Eq 1 mechanism] China withdrawing from exports is the typical outcome, not a special feature of Eq 1. Median Chinese exports in 2040 are 11 GW (planner 384 GW) and fall below 20 GW in 15 of 26 profiles. What makes Eq 1 extreme is that APAC also exports nothing from 2030 onward (median 103 GW in 2040), so no low-cost exporter replaces China.

[Results / capacity metric] Eq 1 has the lowest total capacity in 2040 (792 GW, rank 1/26) and the highest 2040 price, but ranks 13/26 in horizon-average capacity because it holds a lot of capacity in 2030 (rank 24/26). The trapezoidal horizon average in the scatter hides this timing. It also includes the exogenous 2025 capacity (1,401 GW, weight 1/6), a constant offset of about 234 GW.

[Results / welfare] Eq 1, the most extreme outcome in prices, ranks only 14/26 in global welfare (-2.69% vs median -2.58%). High prices are mostly transfers from CS to PS, and Eq 1 holds the least idle capacity (59 GW in 2040). Across profiles, welfare rises with idle capacity (Spearman rho = 0.69 with 2040 idle capacity). Eq 2 has the second-most idle capacity (226 GW) and the highest welfare (-0.84%).

[Results / welfare denominator] CORRECTED. The welfare values are discounted at 2%. The EPEC runs and the welfare post-processing both use RunConfig discount_rate = 0.02. However, the planner benchmark in outputs/llp_planner/ was optimised with discount_rate = 0.0 (solve.log, beta_t all 1.0) and only evaluated at 2%. It is therefore not exactly the welfare maximiser under the metric used for comparison. The planner should be re-solved at 2%, and the parametrization section should state r_d = 2%. The relative losses (0.8-3.6%) are measured against a total dominated by gross consumer utility (inverse-demand intercept a is about 2.1x WTP). Measured against the planner's total manufacturing cost (about 1,553 bn USD undiscounted over the horizon), the median loss of 261 bn USD is roughly 15-17%.

[Equilibrium validity / audit] A targeted non-local deviation check (outputs/eq1_deviation_check_20260928_104717) rejects 11 of the 26 reported profiles at the 1% tolerance, including Eq 1 (China +3.4%, APAC +11.6%). The one-start SLSQP audit starts from the candidate's own offers and cannot cross the zero-flow threshold of routes whose offer lies above the destination price. Many winning deviations are clean 1 USD/kW undercuts that leave the importer price unchanged, i.e. pure volume gains. The paper's verification step needs targeted undercutting tests or multi-start best responses.

[Equilibrium mechanism / ROW] A second, non-obvious type of profitable deviation is fringe entry that breaks another exporter's scarcity rent. In EU-first pf100_k100_a030 (2030), ROW cuts its EU offer to cost and makes zero margin on the 53 GW it exports. It still gains 2.0%, because it becomes the price-setter in the EU. This collapses APAC's capacity rent (about 104 to 7 USD/kW), and all importing prices fall by about 96 USD/kW, including ROW's own import price. ROW's gain is entirely consumer surplus. Both price vectors are uniquely pinned by a non-binding marginal unit, so this is not a dual-degeneracy artefact.

[Model / demand] Demand equals D^max in every region, period and equilibrium, and every observed price is below the regional WTP. The calibrated elasticity (|eps| = 0.9) therefore never binds. Strategic sellers face no demand response, only competition from other suppliers.

[Equilibrium validity / Eq 1] China's withdrawal in Eq 1 is hard to explain as a best response. Offers are cleared period by period, so nothing after 2040 is at stake, and China has no foreign sales whose price it would be protecting. Its 2040 offer to the EU plus shipping (about 511 USD/kW) lies only about 32 USD/kW above the price-setting ROW offer (479 USD/kW), while 37 GW of Chinese capacity sit idle. A rough estimate of the gain from undercutting (37 GW x ~370 USD/kW margin x 5 years, about 68 bn USD) is about 1.7% of China's objective (4,119 bn USD), above the 1% tolerance. The one-start audit nevertheless reports a gain of only 0.11% for China. A likely reason is that an offer above the clearing price receives no flow, so the local solver sees a zero gradient and does not find the discrete undercutting move. This needs a targeted deviation test before Eq 1 is used in the paper.

[Results / 15 profiles] With the 15 surviving profiles, price dispersion across equilibria peaks in 2030-2035 and narrows toward 2040. The EU range is 191-398 USD/kW in 2030 against 166-275 in 2040. The 2040 price-spike narrative therefore disappears. The new Eq 1 (CH-first pf080_k100_a030) produces the highest 2035 prices in all four importing regions (298-328 USD/kW) and has the lowest global welfare (-3.2%). China exports nothing in 2030-2035 and returns in 2040 with 99 GW to the EU, after which Eq 1 has the lowest EU price of all 15. Eq 1 and Eq 2 differ only in their initial offer factor (0.8 vs 1.2), with the same order, capacity path and damping.

[Model / cross-hauling] Because export revenue is valued at the destination clearing price, an export offer acts only as a dispatch bid, and offers below the exporter's own cost cost it nothing. This produces cross-hauling. In Eq 1 in 2035, the EU ships 44 GW to the US while the US ships 67 GW to the EU, both at offers of 0.7-0.8 times their own cost, for 119 GW of cross-hauled volume. Across the 15 profiles cross-hauling is small in the median (6 GW in 2030, 0 later) but appears in 10 of 15 profiles. It wastes shipping cost and is a likely reviewer question. Economically, two-way trade in an identical good can be an equilibrium outcome (reciprocal dumping, Brander & Krugman 1983). A direct withdrawal test on the new Eq 1 confirms that most cross-hauled flows are best responses, because withdrawing US->EU 2030 costs the US 3.4% and EU->US 2035 costs the EU 0.2%. However, withdrawing US->EU 2035 gains the US 0.88%, and withdrawing US->EU 2035 together with US->ROW 2040 gains 1.16% (8.2 bn USD). The new Eq 1 therefore also fails the 1% test. The earlier screen only tested undercutting into zero-flow routes, so "withdraw" deviations (raising the offer on a positive flow) must be added to the verification.

[Model / Africa] Africa's capacity hits its expansion bound (G_exp = 2.5 GW/period) in 54-69% of profiles (2040 range 10.2-10.9 GW, demand 28 GW). Its 543 USD/kW price in Eq 1, against a domestic cost of 96, is therefore largely a scarcity rent created by this exogenous bound.

[Results / Eq 1 scarcity mechanism] In the new Eq 1 (CH-first pf080_k100_a030), every non-Chinese supplier runs at full capacity in 2030 (APAC, EU, US, ROW, AF all binding), while China holds 127 GW idle behind export offers of 3.9-4.1x its cost. No active offer sets the importer prices in 2030. All landed offers lie 62-296 USD/kW below the clearing prices, and the gaps are the exporters' capacity rents (ROW's 62 USD/kW rent links the EU, US, AF and ROW prices). The EU price of 398 USD/kW is therefore a scarcity price, bounded above only by China's landed EU offer (about 436 USD/kW). In 2035 the same pattern holds except that APAC keeps 73 GW idle and sets the EU price with a 19 GW slice. By 2040 prices are again set by marginal offers (China in the EU, APAC in AF, ROW and US), so they fall close to cost. China does not enter in 2030-2035 because large-scale entry ends the scarcity and lowers importer prices by 67-150 USD/kW. The best tested Chinese deviations reach 0.7-0.9%, just below the 1% tolerance, so Eq 1 is a fragile epsilon-equilibrium (Bertrand-Edgeworth logic). Note also that because prices in 2030 are not pinned by any offer, their level lies in a dual-degenerate interval, which the leader's MPEC may select optimistically.

[Results / Eq 1 who gains] Withholding in Eq 1 does not pay off for the withholders. China and APAC rank 12/15 and 15/15 in regional welfare (APAC PS 95 vs median 219 bn USD, China PS 55 vs 110). The scarcity rents go to the capacity-constrained fringe. ROW has the highest welfare of all 15 profiles (PS 86 vs median 0.1 bn USD), and the US, Africa and the EU rank 2, 2 and 5 in PS. Neither China nor APAC recoups the rents later, because by 2040 prices are set by marginal offers close to cost. This is consistent with Eq 1 being a fragile epsilon-equilibrium in which no withholder gains from its own restraint, but no single entry move gains more than 1%.

[Results / median price vs own cost] Median equilibrium prices do not generally sit at the importer's own manufacturing cost. Only ROW's does (median gap -0.4 and -0.3 USD/kW in 2035 and 2040), apart from China's, which is fixed by construction. The EU lies 43-51 USD/kW above its cost in 2030-2040, the US 15-35, APAC 33-69 and Africa 110-155. Exporters price up to the next competing offer with spare capacity, which equals the importer's own cost only where domestic capacity is large enough to be that competitor.

[Equilibrium validity / joint deviations - CRITICAL] A greedy multi-route offer deviation (capacity fixed, 11-point grid per route between own cost and destination price minus shipping, two coordinate sweeps, same market re-clearing and objective as check.py) rejects 13 of the 15 retained profiles at 1%. Only ch-first pf120_k100_a030 (max gain 0.91%) and pf100_k100_a030 (1.00%) survive. In Eq 1, China gains 4.08% by entering all import markets jointly (2030 exports 0 -> 236 GW, EU price 398 -> 206) and APAC gains 3.98%. The single-route undercutting screen misses this, because each market's slice is below 1% while the sum is not. Eq 1's 2030-2035 scarcity is therefore not an equilibrium outcome but an artefact of zero-flow offers having no gradient in the NLP best response. Script and results are in outputs/multiroute_deviation_check_20260928/.

[Results / 14-profile set] The paper now reports 14 profiles (outputs/paper_plots/14_equilibria). The former Eq 1 (CH-first pf080_k100_a030) is excluded because jointly withdrawing US->EU 2035 and US->ROW 2040 raises the US objective by 1.16% (0.88% for the single route, re-verified). The new Eq 1 is AF-first pf100_k050_a040 (888 GW, 179 USD/kW, lowest welfare), and Eq 2 is unchanged (998 GW, 155 USD/kW, highest welfare). The two differ in all four search settings. Removing the old Eq 1 strengthens the capacity-price relation (slope -10.3 USD/kW per 100 GW, R2 0.44 vs 0.28) and the link between welfare and China's 2040 capacity share (rho 0.96 vs 0.87). With manufacturing-cost lines in the price bands, ROW's median price equals its own cost from 2030 onward. EU and US medians lie 23-44 USD/kW above domestic cost and Africa's more than 110, where median 2040 domestic capacity falls short of demand (EU 47/99, US 52/75, AF 11/28 GW).

[Results / prices vs domestic capacity, per-profile check] In 8 of 14 profiles ROW imports its entire 2040 demand (89 GW) at 171-187 USD/kW, just at or below its own cost of 187, while 21-63 GW of ROW capacity stay idle. This fits the idea that domestic capacity caps import offers even without producing. EU and Africa run domestic capacity at full utilization in nearly all profiles but cover at most 62% and 39% of 2040 demand (expansion limits), so imports set the price. Anomaly to check before writing mechanism claims: some observations show idle domestic capacity with a price far above domestic cost (e.g. AF 2040, 4 profiles, output 0 at 167-249 vs cost 96; US 2035 EU-pf080, 37 GW idle at 236 vs cost 188), which contradicts merit-order dispatch with domestic offers at cost (possible reporting mismatch or degenerate duals).
