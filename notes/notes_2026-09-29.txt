[Results / China-APAC export roles, 14 profiles] China remains the largest manufacturer in every equilibrium (2040: 342-590 GW vs APAC 199-289 GW), mainly because it serves its own 316 GW market. In 2040, however, APAC exports more than China in all 14 profiles (APAC median about 134 GW, China 0-111 GW). APAC's lead holds for every update order, price factor, capacity weight and damping factor. China's export level depends strongly on the update order: the median in 2040 is 90 GW when China updates first, 17 GW for EU-first and 0 for AF-first. China's median export offer is 2.0-4.2 times its cost in 2040, against 1.1-2.5 for APAC (China's markup is higher in 14/14). APAC's cost is only 15% above China's in every period. Pattern: this is consistent with a dominant firm/fringe logic. China, with large volume at stake, prices high and holds an umbrella, and APAC undercuts, sells to capacity and expands (planner APAC 33 GW).

[Equilibrium validity / China withdrawal] The less China exports in 2030-2040, the more it gains from the multi-route re-entry deviation (Spearman rho=-0.62, p=0.02). In the only two profiles that survive the multi-route check (CH-first pf120_k100_a030 = Eq 2, and pf100_k100_a030), China exports about 110 GW in 2040, almost as much as APAC (119-135 GW). The extent of China's export withdrawal, and thus the size of APAC's lead, is therefore partly a search artefact: offers above the destination price receive no flow and no gradient. The robust finding is that APAC is at least an equal exporter, not that China exits.

[Results / supplier shares, 14 profiles] Mean supplier shares (plots/plot_supplier_shares_14.py): China's share of served demand falls between 2025 and 2040 from 39% to 4% in the EU, 40% to 5% in the US, 57% to 15% in Africa and 56% to 26% in ROW. APAC reaches 38-58% of these four markets in 2040. Domestic producers cover 46% of EU and 48% of US demand in 2040 (16% and 7% in 2025). China and APAC supply their own markets almost entirely in every period. In the four importing markets, China and APAC together deliver 139 GW in 2025 vs 161 GW in 2040, but the split moves from 79/60 GW to 35/126 GW (means).

[Results / China vs APAC exports by update order, 14 profiles] China exports more than APAC in 11/14 profiles in 2025, 5/14 in 2030 and 0/14 in 2035 and 2040. Over the horizon (cumulative 2025-2040), China leads in only 2/14 profiles, both CH-first (pf120_k050_a030 and pf120_k100_a030 = Eq 2). AF-first and EU-first searches never give China the lead. The two CH-first k100_a030 profiles are also the only ones in which China's multi-route re-entry gain stays below 1% (0.3-0.4%). The other 12 give 1.2-3.5%. In the robust profiles China keeps about 110 GW of exports in 2040, close to APAC (119-135 GW). The paper now attributes strong Chinese withdrawal to update order plus the local gradient-based (IPOPT) best responses, where an offer above the clearing price has zero flow and zero gradient.

[Results / China vs APAC export revenue, 14 profiles] Export revenue and margin are valued at destination clearing prices (the model's producer term). Median annual export margin in bn USD/yr, China vs APAC: 12.4 vs 6.6 (2025), 7.7 vs 8.1 (2030), 2.7 vs 10.2 (2035), 1.1 vs 10.2 (2040). Revenue is 27.8 vs 18.7 in 2025 and 2.2 vs 25.1 in 2040. China earns a higher margin per unit exported than APAC in 36 of 43 profile-years in which both export (median 87-134 vs 77-109 USD/kW), but it loses volume. Discounted 2025-2040 export margin: China median 117 bn USD (2-182) vs APAC 149 bn USD (121-206). China's total is higher in only 3/14 profiles. Key point for defending/qualifying China's strategy: China's welfare is perfectly rank-correlated with its export margin across the 14 profiles (Spearman rho = 1.0). China is best off in the CH-first profiles where it exports most (Eq 2: margin 182 bn, welfare 4,145 bn) and worst off where it withdraws (2 bn, 3,973 bn). Withdrawal is therefore not a better strategy for China, consistent with the multi-route re-entry gains and the gradient-artefact explanation.

[Results / ROW idle capacity as price ceiling, 14 profiles] ROW decommissions 160-260 GW in 2025-2030 in every profile and then holds a median of 61 GW. It imports its entire demand in 10/14 profiles in 2030, 8/14 in 2035 and 8/14 in 2040. In every import-all case the ROW price lies at or below ROW's own manufacturing cost (gap -21 to 0 USD/kW, 2040 -17 to -0.2). Where ROW produces domestically, the price is 0-22 USD/kW above cost (up to +58 in one 2030 case). ROW's price therefore tracks its own cost in every equilibrium, unlike EU/US (+23-44) and AF (>+110). Interpretation: idle domestic capacity at cost acts as an outside option, and exporters price at or just below it (limit pricing). Caveats: (1) The price also sits at cost when ROW's capacity covers only 24-40% of demand, which a pure capacity cap cannot explain (the uniform price would then be set by the residual import offer), so exporter competition (China and APAC both supply ROW) contributes. (2) The capacity is weakly held. In the one-start audit ROW's largest deviation is further 2025 decommissioning in 11/14 profiles (gains 0.03-0.53%). The ceiling works because exporters take ROW's capacity as given (simultaneous moves), not because ROW commits to it.

[What varies across the 14 equilibria: China exports vs importer self-supply, not APAC]
Across the 14 equilibria (horizon averages 2025-2040, Spearman rho, descriptive), APAC's exports (68-127 GW) and capacity are unrelated to the average price (rho 0.04 and 0.01) and only weakly to total capacity (0.24, 0.15). The price instead moves with China's exports (rho -0.78) and with domestic output in the four importing regions (rho +0.76), which substitute almost one for one (rho between them -0.95). APAC's role as external supplier is thus robust, and equilibrium selection mainly decides whether China or the importers' own higher-cost plants serve the remaining demand.
Update (figure plots/plot_price_drivers_14.py -> price_supply_drivers): with the paper's horizon weights (1/6, 1/3, 1/3, 1/6) the values are China exports -0.76, importer domestic output +0.82, APAC exports +0.05 (75-136 GW) against the average price, and -0.93 between China exports and importer output. Use these weighted values in the paper.

[Export offers vs price across the 14 equilibria]
Using the median export offer over the five foreign destinations per period (horizon weighted, because offers above the destination price carry no flow and can be arbitrarily high), China's offer rises with the average price (rho +0.69, 204-380 $/kW) while APAC's offer is only weakly related (rho +0.35, 135-326 $/kW). China's offer and China's export volume carry largely the same information (rho -0.91). China's median offer always lies above the average price range (155-179 $/kW), so it mostly acts as a ceiling behind which APAC and domestic producers supply.

[APAC runs at full load, idle Chinese capacity does not lower prices]
APAC's utilisation is 95-100% (horizon weighted) in all 14 equilibria, so its exports are capacity-bound, which explains why they barely vary and are unrelated to price (rho -0.02). Idle Chinese capacity (103-226 GW) is not associated with higher prices: China's utilisation rises slightly with price (rho +0.40), because high-price equilibria are those where China holds less capacity and exports less, not those where it withholds more. China's share of all cross-border imports into the EU, the US, Africa, and ROW (0-52%, horizon weighted) has rho -0.77 with price, about the same as China's export volume (-0.76). The earlier -0.86 used only Chinese plus APAC deliveries as denominator and should not be cited.
