Served demand equals Dmax in every region, period and equilibrium (E1-E3), with
deviations of order 1e-8. Regional WTP intercepts (a_i = 1100-2400 $/kW) sit far
above equilibrium prices (86-292 $/kW), so the linear inverse-demand branch never
binds and the elasticity calibration (|eps|=0.90) affects only consumer-surplus
accounting, not quantities. Equilibrium multiplicity is therefore entirely a
supply-side phenomenon: who produces, at what offer, with what capacity. This is
also why the three equilibria differ in global welfare by only 0.12% while
regional payoffs differ by up to 7.6% (APAC) - strategic pricing is close to a
pure transfer, and the only real efficiency losses are inefficient production
allocation and the cost of holding idle capacity.

Across E1-E3 the following outcomes are invariant and can be reported as robust
regardless of equilibrium selection: China's domestic clearing price equals its
manufacturing cost exactly in all periods (pinned by the domestic-offer
constraint 9f); EU and US collapse to zero or near-zero manufacturing capacity by
2030; import-dependent regions clear 20-30% above their own post-learning
manufacturing cost from 2030 onward; and global capacity exceeds global demand in
every period. What is NOT invariant is the identity of the secondary supplier and
the location of the capacity overhang: APAC expands to 146-196 GW in E2/E3 but
collapses to 27 GW in E1, while ROW retains 99 GW (E1), 44 GW (E2) or 19 GW (E3)
at exactly 0% utilization throughout. The "idle capacity concentrated in ROW"
claim survives qualitatively but its magnitude varies by a factor of five.

E3 shows China holding 210-219 GW idle at 69-71% utilization through 2030-2035
while APAC expands into the EU and US markets, then moving to 99.3% utilization
in 2040 and recapturing 89-100% of every import market, with APAC capacity
falling from 196 GW to 13.5 GW. The 2040 APAC collapse cannot be cleanly
attributed to strategy, because 2045 is the terminal period, carries no salvage
value, and is a reporting buffer only; an end-of-horizon pull toward
decommissioning is present and confounded with the strategic effect. Separating
them requires extending the buffer (a 2050 period, or a nonzero
terminal_capacity_value) and checking whether APAC still exits at 2040.

The uncontaminated evidence for the strategic-buffer claim is ROW, not APAC. ROW
holds capacity at exactly 0% utilization in every period including the terminal
one, paying 0.56-1.37 bn USD per period in fixed holding costs for capacity that
ships nothing (E3: 18.5 GW; E2: 43.5 GW). End-of-horizon effects push toward
scrapping, so a region that pays to retain wholly idle capacity into the terminal
period is evidence for, not against, the outside-option interpretation. In E1
ROW instead activates its retained capacity in 2040 at 100% utilization, which is
the same option exercised rather than merely held.

The MPEC and the planner do not optimize over the same horizon. The per-player
MPEC objective in model_main.py sums over the full set T including 2045, while
the planner objective in model_llp_planner.py sums over T_plan and explicitly
excludes 2045 as a terminal buffer. The regional objective values stored in the
acceptance audits therefore include a 2045 term and are not on the 2025-2040
reporting basis used for the manuscript's welfare table. Any EPEC-versus-planner
welfare gap and any welfare-based equilibrium selection must be recomputed on a
common period set before being reported; the 0.12% spread across E1-E3 is small
enough that the selection rule could flip. All four capacity policy levers
(cap_keep_reward, capex_subsidy, terminal_capacity_value, decommission_penalty)
default to zero in RunConfig, so the statement that the model contains no salvage
term is accurate as run.

The 28-branch direct zero-proximal grid (80-sweep cap, 7 player orders x 2 damping
x 2 initializations) yielded exactly one accepted profile at 0.9965% (APAC,
af-eu-us-apac-row-ch/high_a040, sweep 10); 17 branches exhausted 80 sweeps and 10
died on best-response solver failures. The proximal-stabilized Stage-1 anchors
plus short unpenalized release sweeps produced three acceptances within 5-7
sweeps. This is the cleanest available evidence that the proximal scheme is a
basin-finding device rather than part of the equilibrium definition, since every
acceptance test is run at zero proximal penalty in the original economic game.
The postrun analysis itself cautions that a matched pre-registered experiment is
needed before claiming penalties are necessary.

Two defects in the current manuscript are confirmed by these artifacts. First, the
profile reported in the paper is not an equilibrium: its frozen zero-proximal
audit gives a 47.58% unilateral gain for ROW, with CH, APAC, US and AF also above
1%. Second, all three historical "converged" runs behind the current figures used
b_dem slopes inconsistent with the workbook's own formula (23 of 24 coefficients
wrong, Africa 2025 the only match). Consequently Table I, the 1.05% planner gap,
and the capacity trajectory (paper: 1401 -> 1290 GW; corrected E1-E3: 1401 -> 748-783
GW in 2040) all need regeneration from the corrected-demand artifacts, and the
planner benchmark must be re-solved on the corrected workbook.

Base-year vintage of the demand calibration is internally inconsistent with the
introduction, and this is checkable without leaving the PDF. The intro states
2024 actuals of >550 GW global, 324 GW China, ~50 GW US and ~15 GW Africa, while
Table I gives 2025 Dmax of 500, 280, 45 and 5 GW for the same regions - the 2025
period is below the stated 2024 outturn everywhere except the EU, and Africa is
off by a factor of three. China's 280 is almost certainly the 2024 NEA
grid-connected figure (277.6 GW), so the paper carries two different numbers for
China 2024 that differ by 15% on different accounting bases. The available
defence is that periods are five-year blocks (y_t = 5), so Dmax_2025 is the
representative annual demand for 2025-2029 rather than calendar 2025; under IEA
outlooks Chinese annual additions decline after the mid-decade peak, which makes
a block average below the 2024 peak defensible. Africa is not rescued by that
relabelling and needs either a higher Dmax or a corrected intro sentence.

Two provenance defects compound this. First, the only record of the demand
derivation in the workbook is a `research` sheet citing "Renewables 2025,
2025-2030, from regional summaries" with China 420, EU 80, APAC 100, US 50,
Africa 7.8, ROW 128 (sum 786), which matches neither the 2025 column (500) nor
the 2030 column (670); the tabulated values are therefore not traceable to the
cited source, which is precisely what Reviewer 2's transparency request asks
for. Second, the introduction attributes China's ~930 GW of module capacity -
also Table I's initial capacity, a core model input - to iea_pv_supply, the IEA
Solar PV Global Supply Chains special report of 2022, whose vintage cannot
support that number. Results are not at risk: demand binds in every region and
period, but capacity is 1401 GW against 500 GW of demand (2.8x oversupply, still
2.4x at +15% demand), so the exit ordering, the capacity cliff and the strategic
capacity-holding result are insensitive to the base-year level.

Eq. (9f), pinning the domestic self-offer to marginal cost, is a necessary
normalization rather than the redundancy Reviewer 2 suspects, and the argument is
exact here. Collecting the terms in region r's own objective that multiply its own
market-clearing price gives -lambda_r * x_dem_r from CS and +(lambda_r - c_man_r) *
x_flow_{r,r} from the i=r term of PS, so dW_r/dlambda_r = x_flow_{r,r} - x_dem_r =
-(net imports of r). A self-sufficient region therefore has its own domestic offer
drop out of its objective entirely, leaving a flat direction in the strategy vector
and an unbounded MPEC solution set in that coordinate; because served demand equals
Dmax in every region and period, quantities cannot respond and the cancellation is
exact, not first-order. China is exactly self-sufficient (net imports 0.0) in every
period of all three equilibria, so without (9f) the dominant player's domestic offer
is completely undetermined and ||Delta theta|| can never settle. For a net importer
the derivative is strictly negative, so the region would offer domestically below
its own marginal cost to depress lambda_r, which in the LLP merit order dispatches
domestic supply ahead of genuinely cheaper imports - a modelling artifact rather
than market power. This also matches Reviewer 1's reading that markups are an
export-side instrument.

Eqs. (9d) and (9e) are both genuinely non-binding and should be conceded. The
workbook sets g_dec_ub_per_yr = 1.0 for all six regions and the code implements
Dcap_neg <= g_dec * Kcap, a fraction of stock per year, so over a five-year period
the bound permits retiring five times the installed stock; the effective
restriction is capacity non-negativity. The worst usage observed across E1-E3 is
Dcap_neg/Kcap = 0.2000 (EU 2025, 4.4 GW/yr on 22 GW), which is exactly 1/y_t, i.e.
complete exit within a single period, at 20% of the stated bound. The offer-price
ceiling is 2000 $/kW uniformly while the largest offer realized anywhere in E1-E3
is 353-364 $/kW, also below every regional choke price p_full (521-1136). Note that
printed Eq. (9d) states an absolute lower bound on Delta K while the code enforces a
proportional one, so deleting (9d) resolves both the redundancy and the
notation error at once.

The centralized benchmark already is what Reviewer 2 asks for: model_llp_planner.py
declares x, x_dem, Kcap, Icap_pos and Dcap_neg as decision variables and maximizes a
single objective, so it is a single-level planner jointly optimizing capacity and
flows, not the LLP conditioned on EPEC capacities. Only the page-7 wording is
misleading. It must be corrected together with the horizon asymmetry, since the
planner sums over T_plan (excluding 2045) while each MPEC sums over the full T.
