[Eq 1-3 - equilibria are distinguished by whether China is active in export markets]
The three highlighted equilibria differ mainly in China's export offers. In Eq 2 (highest capacity and welfare), China offers at 1.6-2.5x its manufacturing cost, exports about 110 GW per period and keeps 590 GW. In Eq 1 (lowest capacity and welfare), China offers at 4-10x cost into every foreign market and exports nothing in any period; it shrinks to 336 GW, just enough for its own demand. EU, US and ROW then build domestic capacity (61, 97 and 139 GW in 2040), and ROW becomes the swing supplier to Europe. China's domestic price is identical in all three because it is pinned at cost, so all differences come from the export side.

[Eq 1-3 - idle capacity is not what makes an equilibrium inefficient]
Global idle capacity (2030-2040) is lowest in Eq 1 (43-46 GW), the least efficient equilibrium, and highest in Eq 2 (202-239 GW), the most efficient one. The welfare loss is driven by who produces, not by how much capacity stands idle. When China withdraws, high-cost regional production replaces low-cost Chinese output. Holding idle capacity is comparatively cheap (capacity costs differ by only a few bn USD per region). ROW again holds 63 GW at 0% utilization in every period of Eq 2, consistent with the outside-option interpretation.

[Methodology - a "withdrawn" China may be a local rather than global best response]
In Eq 1 China's export offers carry no flows, so they are payoff-irrelevant locally, and re-entering exports would require China to cut offers and rebuild capacity at the same time. A local unilateral-deviation test starting from this state may not find such a joint move. China earns 174 bn USD (4.4%) more in Eq 2 than in Eq 1, but opponents' strategies differ, so this does not prove a profitable deviation. A cross-play check (China's best response against the Eq 1 opponents, started from China's Eq 2 strategy) would test whether Eq 1 survives a non-local deviation.

[Prices] Median prices fall over time in every region, but the planner price falls just as fast because of manufacturing-cost learning. The decline alone is therefore not a strategic result. The strategic finding is that the gap does not close. The global demand-weighted median is 222/164/154/142 USD/kW (2025/30/35/40) vs the planner's 169/114/103/96, so the absolute gap stays at about 50 USD/kW and the ratio rises from 1.32 to 1.48. Regionally, China stays at planner and cost (pinned by construction). APAC converges to planner by 2040 (median/planner 1.45 -> 1.04). EU, US, ROW and AF stay at about 1.8-2.4x planner through 2040.

[Prices] ROW's median price equals ROW's own manufacturing cost from 2030 on (233/207/187), so ROW self-supplies at cost. EU and US medians sit above their own manufacturing cost (EU 2040: 222 vs cost 159; US: 203 vs 170). AF has the highest markup over planner (about 2.3x) and imports about 60% of its demand. Every regional price maximum (EU 571, AF 566, US 462, ROW 398) comes from Eq 1, where China withdraws from exports.

[Prices, Eq 1 mechanism] From the LLP KKT conditions, lambda_i = offer_e,i + ship_e,i + mu_e for every active flow, and mu_i = lambda_i - c_i for domestic supply. In Eq 1, China's export offers are out of the market. Almost all non-Chinese capacity then runs at full utilization, and EU, US and AF expand at (or near) their g_exp limits (EU +13 GW and AF +2.5 GW per 5-year period). Prices are set by the remaining exporters' strategic offers, e.g. EU 2030: ROW's offer plus shipping of 529 gives lambda_EU = 537. Domestic producers earn large scarcity rents (lambda - c of 250-450 USD/kW in EU, US and AF). US 2040 shows the reverse: US capacity is no longer binding (util 0.8), and the US price falls back to US cost (170). In Eq 2, China is the marginal supplier in EU, AF and ROW, prices equal China's landed offer, and EU and US rents are only 10-40 USD/kW. So the high Eq 1 prices are largely a transfer from domestic consumers to domestic producers, which limits the welfare loss.

[Equilibrium set revised to 26] The former Eq 1 (eu-us-af-row-apac-ch/pf120_k050_a040) was removed because it is not actually an equilibrium, so the notes above on Eq 1 are outdated. Across the 26 profiles:
- Median global welfare is 9,853.5 bn USD (-2.6%), with losses of 0.8-3.6%.
- The sign of the regional effects is now fully invariant: CH and APAC gain in 26/26, and EU, US, AF and ROW lose in 26/26.
- The four other regions lose 453 bn USD vs a CH+APAC gain of 203 bn USD.
- Spearman rho is 0.75 (China vs the other regions' welfare) and 0.85 (global welfare vs China's 2040 capacity share).
- The OLS slope is -12 USD/kW per 100 GW, with R2 = 0.33.
The new Eq 1 is ch-af-apac-eu-row-us/pf080_k100_a040 (909 GW, 181 USD/kW, the highest average price). It shares the update order and capacity weight with Eq 2 and differs only in price factor (0.8 vs 1.2) and damping (0.4 vs 0.3). The new Eq 1 produces all 2040 regional price maxima (EU 479, AF 543, US 357, ROW 315). Mechanism: China exports at 1.6-3x cost until 2030 and sets most prices (EU 228 in 2030). From 2035 China offers 4-6x cost and withdraws. Domestic producers then earn scarcity rents of 130-450 USD/kW by 2040. Its welfare (9,842.6 bn) is close to the median despite the highest prices, which is consistent with an intra-regional CS-to-PS transfer.

[Why EU prices exceed EU manufacturing cost] EU capacity is fully utilized in essentially all 26 equilibria but covers only about 55% of 2040 demand (median 54 GW vs Dmax 99 GW). The marginal unit is therefore always imported, and lambda_EU follows the strategic import offers (China/APAC), not EU cost. The difference (median lambda - c of about 62 USD/kW in 2040) is a scarcity rent to EU producers. The effect is structural: even at the expansion limit (2.67 GW/yr, so 62 GW in 2040), the EU could cover at most about 63% of its 2040 demand. The rent size follows the domestic coverage gap: AF (cap/demand 0.39, median rent 123), EU (0.55, 62), US (0.94, 37).

[Epsilon caveat on capacities] In the Stage 2 audits, the most profitable unilateral deviation for the EU is additional capacity in almost every profile, often exactly up to its expansion limit. The same holds for the US in most profiles. The gains are only 0.01-0.8% (EU) and 0.1-1.0% (US) of regional welfare, below the 1% tolerance. Reported EU/US capacities therefore sit somewhat below exact best responses. This does not change the price mechanism, since even maximal EU capacity cannot displace imports, but the capacity section or the limitations should state it.

[Price vs trade/competition, 26 profiles] The horizon-average demand-weighted price falls with trade openness, all Spearman rho significant at p<0.01:
- China's exports: rho=-0.61
- Cross-border trade share of demand: -0.57
- Number of market-years where China and APAC both supply the same market: -0.58
- APAC's exports: -0.50
Market concentration (HHI) and the number of foreign suppliers are not significant. Controlling for China's exports (partial rank correlation), the trade share (-0.17), CH-APAC co-presence (-0.22) and APAC's exports (-0.32) all become insignificant, while China's exports remain significant controlling for APAC (-0.50). So the driver is whether China exports, not trade or competition per se. By price tercile, China exports 73 vs 31 vs 23 GW/yr and the trade share is 27% vs 20% vs 19%. China and APAC rarely supply the same market (median 1 of 16 market-years). The new Eq 1 is an exception on horizon averages (China exports 63 GW/yr, but APAC only 15, and China withdraws from 2035), so timing matters.
